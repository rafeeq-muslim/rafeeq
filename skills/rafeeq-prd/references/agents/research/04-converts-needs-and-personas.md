# Who new Muslims are and what they need: evidence for personas and the first-year journey

Research note 04 for Rafeeq (رفيق). Prepared 4 October 2026. Evidence base: web research across academic, institutional, ministry and press sources; full texts were read where available and marked SNIP where only a summary or abstract was seen. Every source has a number [S#] resolved in section 8 with URL and date.

**How to read this note**

- Reliability tags: **PR** peer-reviewed; **INST** report by a research institute, NGO or dawah body; **GOV** ministry or official statistic (usually seen via press); **PRESS** journalism; **OPIN** opinion or practitioner guidance; **SNIP** I saw only a search summary or abstract, not the full text (treat as unverified).
- Three limits to keep in mind when building personas:
  1. Almost all rigorous convert research is Western (UK, US, Australia). Gulf evidence is mostly administrative counts and press. The Osool 2015 survey [S1] has n=114, 62% from the UK, about 90% from UK/Europe/North America, 65% women, and interviews done through preachers and centres. It does **not** describe the typical Saudi expat convert.
  2. No validated retention data exists. Figures such as "70% of converts leave" circulate without data [S30]. Do not use them.
  3. Where a timeline phase below is labelled "inferred", it is my synthesis of several sources, not a measured stage.

## 1. Executive summary

1. **Scale and data quality.** KSA recorded 347,646 new Muslims in 2019-2023, 163,319 of them in 2023 (+74% on 2022) [S15]. Dubai alone recorded 5,511 in H1 2026 against "more than 3,600" in H1 2025 [S19]; Kuwait's IPC reported 2,702 in the past year [S20]. Saudi Arabia launched a digital "New Muslim" card on Tawakkalna in July 2026, explicitly to improve data quality [S15]. The 2022-23 jump is unexplained and counts are unaudited.
2. **A Gulf convert is not a Western convert.** Non-Saudis are 77% male [S16] and 65% of converts in a 2016 Saudi count were male [S15]; Western samples are 62-65% women [S1][S4]. Nationality depends on the office: about 60% Filipino in Dubai (2018) [S19], 84% in one Qatar Ramadan sample [S21], but 55% Ethiopian or Eritrean in two Riyadh centres (2013) [S15].
3. **The first months are social more than informational.** After telling others, 48% cut a tie, 43% became lonely and 5% were homeless [S1]; 66% of UK converts met initial family negativity and 28% stayed negative [S4]. In the US, parent relations dip then recover above baseline [S2]. Practitioners describe the welcome fading within weeks [S24].
4. **What protects.** Muslim friendships and mosque attachment were the strongest, most consistent predictors of positive outcomes in the largest convert survey [S2]; a Muslim friend was the most-used help in Osool's sample (87% fully or partly effective) [S1 p.57].
5. **Information gaps are measurable.** 66% struggled to keep up rituals; 54% got wrong information from Muslims; 54% found no answers to doubts; about 50% lacked trusted sources or teachers or were confused about sects [S1 p.54]. The internet was the second most-used help but fully effective for 38% (39% partly) [S1 p.57]. This supports an approved-sources assistant with human referral, not a replacement for people.
6. **Doubt is common and mostly social.** 39% of US converts had doubts at least sometimes; 23% had considered leaving. Top doubt topics: gender rules (44%), restricted freedom (37%), intolerance of Muslims (37%); Muslims' conduct fed doubt more than theology [S2].
7. **Risks.** Family rupture; remote converts without community (EU RAN 2021, via press: self-study without community ties raises radicalisation risk [S22]); early-marriage pressure (23%) and discrimination by Muslims (44%) [S1]. Extremism over-representation is contested [S27][S6]. Access is not guaranteed: Amnesty (2025) found confiscated phones and 16-hour days among Kenyan domestic workers in KSA [S17].
8. **Mentors and offices are stretched.** Osool lists too few preachers and translators, weak follow-up, missing language content, no same-nationality preacher, poor coordination and thin funding [S1 p.107]. Practitioner designs cap a mentor at 5-10 converts [S1 p.102]. No convert-specific mentoring-effect study was found; generic youth-mentoring evidence says training and supervision drive effects [S26].

## 2. The first-year journey: timeline of needs

Dates are counted from the shahada. "Measured" means a survey or dataset shows it; "practitioner" means institutional or opinion guidance; "inferred" means my synthesis.

**Stage models in the literature (descriptive, none gives month-by-month timing).** Rambo's seven stages are context, crisis, quest, encounter, interaction, commitment, consequences [S13]. Köse studied 70 native British converts and built a conversion-process model, with a post-conversion chapter on changes and relations with parents and society [S13]. Roald (2012) describes four post-conversion stages: zealotry, disappointment with the new peer group, acceptance that Muslims are ordinary people, and a more private, "secularised" practice [S12]. Osool's own model runs from receiving the call, through entering Islam, basics of practice and learning, to advanced practice and da'wa, with "stabilising faith" phases against desires, doubts, fatigue and opposition [S1 p.100]. Zebiri found British converts mostly well educated, deciding after long reflection [S13].

### Before the shahada (context for onboarding)
- Conversion is usually slow and knowledge-driven: 99% of US converts say studying Islam influenced them; about 75% describe a "slow and gradual" process; mean age 25 (US) [S2], 27.5 (UK) [S4]. In the Philippine study, 28% cited personal conviction, about 18% a preacher and about 17% work in the Middle East [S10].

### Week 1 (inferred from practitioner and measured items)
| Need | Evidence | Type |
|---|---|---|

| Decide whether and when to tell family or employer | Osool: default is to announce, but safety exceptions apply [S1 p.88]. US data: 39% told their mother and 35% their father before or shortly after; 51% told their spouse then; employers are told last [S2 pp.56-57] | Measured + practitioner |

| Official onboarding in KSA | New digital Muslim card via Tawakkalna from July 2026 (QR code, SDAIA-linked) [S15] | GOV |

| Need | Evidence | Type |
|---|---|---|

### 4.2 Who they are in KSA (what can and cannot be said)
- **Population base.** Non-Saudis are 15.67 million (44% of residents), 77.2% male and 22.8% female, roughly 44% aged 20-39 (GASTAT 2024 estimate; age share computed from the age table by the fetch tool, verify) [S16]. Largest groups in the 2022 census: Bangladesh 2.12m, India 1.88m, Pakistan 1.81m, Yemen 1.80m, Egypt 1.47m, Sudan 0.82m, Philippines 0.73m, Syria 0.45m, Nepal 0.30m (secondary report of census figures) [S16]. Sri Lanka, Ethiopia, Kenya counts were not available.
- **Nationality of converts is office-specific and rarely published.** The ministry did not publish nationality [S15]. Examples: two Riyadh centres, 708 converts (period not stated, 2013): 392 Ethiopians and Eritreans, 146 Sri Lankans, 136 Filipinos, 29 Europeans/Americans, 5 Indians [S15]; Kuwait IPC cumulative: Filipinos 28,541, Indians 15,663, Sri Lankans 15,610, Africans 1,723 (undated) [S20]; Dubai about 60% Filipino (2018) [S19]. An Al-Ahsa study covers converts from the Philippines, Sri Lanka and India, with 11,087 registered by 2014 (SNIP) [S15].
- **Occupations.** 3.74 million non-Saudi domestic workers in Q3 2023, **71.7% male** (drivers, gardeners and similar) and 28.3% female [S16]; Kuwait IPC outreach targets workers in markets, farms, workplaces and deportation centres [S20]. Osool lists drivers and herders as hard-to-reach [S1 p.71]. Occupation data for converts specifically were not found, so do not assume one occupation.
- **Languages.** Dubai summit asked for English, Tagalog and Mandarin education [S19]; Kuwait IPC works in 14 languages [S20]; Osool names missing content "in some convert languages" and no same-nationality preacher [S1 p.107]. Ethiopian/Oromo and Sri Lankan Sinhala/Tamil needs are plausible but not evidenced in a survey.
- **Connectivity.** Population-level internet use is 99% in KSA [S18], but low-wage domestic workers may have phones confiscated, long hours and no day off [S17]. No survey of smartphone ownership or WhatsApp use among low-wage migrant converts was found. Mark as a gap to test.
- **Literacy and education.** No direct data. Philippine returnee converts: 82.6% informal and 10.1% formal Islamic education; 35.8% hold a college degree [S10]. Education of Ethiopian, Kenyan or Sri Lankan converts: not found.
- **Gender of converts.** 65% male in the 2016 Saudi count [S15]; 62-65% female in Western convenience samples [S1][S4]. Design for both.

### 4.3 Returning home
Many Philippine converts converted while working in the Gulf, particularly Saudi Arabia, then returned and formed Balik-Islam centres and networks; OFW returnees became active preachers [S10][S11]. In the Philippines they report hijab discrimination at work, hostile families, halal-access problems, and feeling "no longer fit comfortably in the non-Muslim world and haven't yet found my place in the Muslim world" [S10]. A Rafeeq user can leave KSA within the first year. Journey design should survive relocation.

### 4.4 Online and remote converts
- Providers report chat-based shahadas: iERA 977 in 2020-21 [S22]; AboutIslam 62 in 2022 with a "startup kit" of links, newsletter and WhatsApp/Facebook groups [S22].
- TikTok and Discord converts (US, 2023) move from videos to Quran audiobooks to online book clubs to a public shahada on TikTok Live; exact numbers unknowable [S22].
- Osool: online dialogue offers reach, privacy and anonymity, but loses the ability to check understanding, intimacy and emotional state, and has language gaps (Korean, Spanish), weak connections and moderator skill limits [S1 pp.110-112].

### 4.5 Western comparators (context only)
US: 20-23% of Muslims are converts [S8][S9]; UK: about 100,000, 62% women, mean age 27.5 [S4]; Australia: 15.8% of respondents [S6]. US education figures conflict (ISPU: 15% hold a bachelor's degree; Yaqeen's non-representative sample: typically a bachelor's) [S2][S9].

| Factor | Evidence |

## 6. Mentors and dawah staff: needs and pain points

### 6.1 Dawah offices in KSA (Osool, Saudi model)
Osool's ten listed problems for centres in Muslim countries "with Saudi Arabia as the model": too few preachers and translators; too few programmes for new Muslims; weak cooperation from sponsors and employers; weak mechanisms to contact converts after the shahada or after they drop out; difficulty working with converts who do not practise; missing content in some languages; few government-backed programmes and permit difficulties for translator-preachers; no preacher of the convert's nationality; no money even for small incentives to attract converts to courses; poor coordination and duplicated programmes between centres [S1 p.107]. Related: staff job insecurity and months-long event approvals (a Badr, Medina office director, 2014) [S15]; an Al-Noor (Dammam) office reporting 489 activities for 14,926 beneficiaries but six converts in the month (2019), which suggests outreach-heavy reporting [S15].

**Illustrative arithmetic (derived, not measured):** 163,319 conversions in 2023 divided by 423 foreign preachers is about 386 per preacher; Kuwait's 2,702 divided by 78 preachers is about 35 per preacher. The Saudi figure likely counts through other staff and societies, so use it only to show that caseloads are very unequal across systems, not as a statistic.

### 6.2 Volunteer mentors: designs and constraints
- Osool "buddy" design: one born-Muslim friend per convert; mentor lives within 10 km; start with a minimum set of tasks (purification, prayer, ghusl, hijab, nearest mosque, scholars); cap each mentor at 5 or 10; a database of willing mentors; match ethnicity and accent; visit converts in homes; add them to educational WhatsApp or Facebook groups [S1 p.101-102].
- Group design: separate men and women, 50 per group, one supervisor follows 200 converts through four groups; practitioners claim about 50% success (opinion, no data) [S1 p.102].
- Distance follow-up: a minimum regular contact, even about feelings, matters; a central centre can link each convert to the nearest helper [S1 p.103].
- Boundaries and referral: avoid treating converts as projects; know limits; set boundaries; build peer support; signpost for domestic abuse, mental health, extremism and addiction [S24].
- Burnout: volunteers who say yes to everything exhaust themselves (opinion, May 2026) [S29]; generic mentoring research finds mentor time constraints and lost contact are the main reasons matches close (SNIP) [S32] and that effects grow with training and supervision [S26].
- Effect on converts: **no convert-specific evaluation found**; only correlational support (friendship, belonging) [S2]. A UK Islamic Foundation programme lists services but no outcome data [S28].

### 6.3 What this implies for Rafeeq (hypotheses for interviews)
Staff want fewer repeat questions, a shared record of each convert, language coverage, visible dropout, and a route to give converts a mentor of the same language and gender. Mentors want a small caseload, clear scripts for week 1, a way to escalate sensitive cases, and no 24/7 expectation.

## 7. Persona inputs (DRAFTS built from the evidence; validate in interviews)

Names are placeholders. Each trait carries a source tag; untagged traits are marked **assumption**.

### P1. "Shift-worker convert": non-Arabic-speaking expat worker in KSA
- **Who.** Most likely a man (non-Saudis 77% male; domestic workers 72% male [S16]) or a woman; 20s-30s [S16]; from the Philippines, Sri Lanka, Ethiopia/Eritrea, India or Nepal [S15][S19][S21]; declared at a dawah office, work-site event or Ramadan programme [S15][S20].
- **Situation.** Long shifts and mobile work (driving, herding, farms), little time after work, lessons far away, sponsor unhelpful [S1 pp.71, 107]. A live-in domestic worker may have no phone access or day off [S17].
- **Needs.** Mother-tongue content and a same-nationality contact [S1 p.107]; short daily-practice steps and reassurance that gradual practice is fine [S1 p.92]; proof of status (Tawakkalna card, July 2026) [S15]; a private way to ask questions (assumption).
- **Pain points.** Missing programmes in his language, Arabic-only classes, no follow-up after the declaration [S1 p.107].
- **Channels.** Social use in KSA is led by TikTok, Snapchat, YouTube, Instagram, Facebook [S18]; WhatsApp reported at 92.7% [S5] (SNIP). Voice-first, low-literacy design is an assumption.
- **Future.** May return home within the year (see P6) [S10].

### P2. "Remote searcher": online convert far from any community
- **Who.** 20s-30s [S2][S4]; found Islam through YouTube, TikTok or Quran audio and declared through chat or a live stream [S22]; Western samples are mostly women (62-65%) [S1][S4]; Gulf online users unknown.
- **Needs.** Private, fast answers; a correct way to declare; a human friend; a route to a local mosque or a same-language mentor [S1 pp.110-111][S25].
- **Risks.** Self-study without community raises radicalisation exposure [S22]; the internet fully helped only 38% (39% partly) [S1 p.57]; newer converts with fewer Muslim friends are lonelier at Ramadan and Eid [S2].
- **Behaviour.** Reads heavily (99% say studying influenced them) [S2]; 11% of US converts attend no mosque or third space [S2].

### P3. "Disowned convert": facing family rejection
- **Who.** Any country; most acute for young adults living with family and for women whose husband stays non-Muslim, a stronger pressure than a parent's disapproval per Osool [S1 p.70].
- **What happened.** Relationship dipped after telling; possible expulsion or financial cut-off [S2][S10]; 48% cut a tie, 43% lonely, 5% homeless [S1].
- **Needs.** No one-size rule on when to tell [S2 p.60]; practical help (housing, funds) and mental-health referral [S2 p.7]; calm scripts for family talks and the reminder that relations often recover [S2]; privacy where safety is at risk [S1 p.88].
- **Pain points.** Hiding prayer or fasting; fear of non-Islamic burial [S10]; being pushed by Muslims on secondary issues [S1 p.70].
- **Protective.** One trusted Muslim friend, a mentor who answers fast, a convert fund [S2].

### P4. "Volunteer mentor"
- **Who.** A born Muslim or a convert of some years (assumption) with limited time [S1 p.102][S32].
- **Needs.** Caseload of 5-10 and clear boundaries [S1 p.102][S24]; a week-1 script; matching on language, gender, nationality [S1 p.102]; escalation and signposting for abuse, mental health, extremism, addiction [S24]; peer support [S24]; training and supervision [S26].
- **Pain points.** Burnout from open-ended availability [S29]; losing contact with the convert [S32]; "can't be an expert in everything" [S24].
- **Success.** The convert has a Muslim friend and a mosque link and reaches Ramadan without loneliness [S2].

### P5. "Dawah-office coordinator" (مكتب الدعوة / جمعية توعية الجاليات)
- **Who.** Staff member with few translators and preachers, a diverse caseload and activity-heavy reporting [S1 p.107][S15].
- **Needs.** A record per convert (contact, language, nationality, sponsor issue); reminders; language-matched mentors; visibility of dropouts; a way to log the new Tawakkalna card (assumption on feature) [S1 p.107][S15].
- **Pain points.** Few staff; weak follow-up; no same-nationality preacher; uncooperative sponsors; no funds for incentives; duplication between offices [S1 p.107]; slow approvals and job insecurity [S15].
- **Scale cues.** One office's Hajj programme for new Muslims had 800 participants in its 19th round and 80+ language-speaking supervisors (2009) [S15]; Kuwait IPC runs 881 courses in 14 languages [S20].

### P6. "Returning OFW": converted in the Gulf, now at home
- **Who.** Filipino who converted while working in Saudi Arabia and returned; mean age at conversion about 32 (men) and 36 (women); 17% of the Philippine sample cite Middle East work as the route; may join a Balik-Islam centre or become a preacher [S10].
- **Needs.** Halal access, help with hijab and job discrimination, burial and family-rights guidance, a community that is not cold, and sound teaching that avoids extreme readings [S10][S11].
- **Pain points.** Feels "no longer fit comfortably in the non-Muslim world and haven't yet found my place in the Muslim world"; job loss; security suspicion [S10].

### Open questions to test in interviews
Phone ownership, data cost and literacy among low-wage converts; whether KSA offices already run WhatsApp groups; acceptable weekly mentor time and gender rules in KSA; whether a Gulf-based mentor can serve a convert who leaves KSA; how converts in KSA experience family loss when family is abroad.
